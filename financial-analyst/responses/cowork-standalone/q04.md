# Q04: Analyze Amazon's cash flow — break down free cash flow generation and explain what's driving the difference between net income and operating cash flow.

## Response

Let me pull the most current detail from Amazon's filing to ensure accuracy on the cash flow components.## Q4: Amazon's Cash Flow — The Full Breakdown

This is one of the most important analyses you can do on Amazon, because the company has always had a massive gap between net income and operating cash flow — and in FY2025, that gap is more revealing than ever.

---

### The High-Level Picture

| Metric | FY2025 | FY2024 | YoY Change |
|--------|--------|--------|------------|
| **Net Income** | $77.7B | $59.2B | +31.3% |
| **Operating Cash Flow** | $139.5B | $115.9B | +20.4% |
| **Net Income → OCF Gap** | **$61.8B** | **$56.7B** | +9.0% |
| **Capital Expenditures** | ($128.3B) | ($77.7B) | +65.1% |
| **Free Cash Flow** | **$11.2B** | **$38.2B** | -70.7% |

Amazon generated $139.5B in cash from operations on $77.7B of net income — meaning **$61.8 billion in non-cash adjustments and working capital effects** bridge the gap between GAAP earnings and actual cash generation. Let me break that down.

---

### What Drives the $61.8B Gap Between Net Income and Operating Cash Flow

#### 1. Depreciation & Amortization: ~$65.8B (+24.5% YoY)

This is the single largest reconciling item, and it explains most of the gap by itself. Amazon depreciates its massive and growing fleet of physical assets — data centers, fulfillment centers, delivery vehicles, aircraft, corporate offices, and technology equipment — over their useful lives.

The 24.5% YoY increase directly reflects the cumulative effect of Amazon's infrastructure buildout. Every dollar of the $77.7B spent on capex in FY2024 (and the years before) flows through the income statement as depreciation expense over 5–15 years but has *zero cash impact* in the current period. Meanwhile, the original cash was already spent in prior years. So depreciation is a non-cash expense that reduces net income but gets added back in the cash flow statement.

In Amazon's case, this is particularly large because: the company has been spending $60B–$130B annually on physical infrastructure in recent years; data center equipment (servers, GPUs, networking gear) depreciates on relatively short 3–6 year schedules, so the depreciation curve is steep; and the fulfillment/logistics network (warehouses, delivery stations, vans, aircraft) represents hundreds of billions in cumulative historical investment.

#### 2. Stock-Based Compensation: ~$19.5B

Amazon issues substantial equity compensation to employees, particularly in engineering, product, and senior leadership roles. SBC is a real economic cost (it dilutes shareholders), but it's a non-cash expense — no cash leaves the building. This adds back ~$19.5B to cash flow.

Note the interesting dynamic here: SBC actually *declined* by about 11.6% YoY, which is unusual. This likely reflects Amazon's headcount optimization that began in late 2022/2023 and the fact that some large grants issued during the pandemic hiring boom are now fully vested.

#### 3. Operating Lease Amortization: ~$10–12B (estimated)

Amazon has enormous operating lease obligations — primarily for fulfillment centers, delivery stations, and office space. Under ASC 842, these create right-of-use assets that are amortized as non-cash expense. The cash payment shows up as a reduction to the lease liability (partially in operating activities, partially in financing), but the amortization adds back to operating cash flow. This is a significant and often overlooked reconciling item for Amazon.

#### 4. Deferred Income Taxes: Variable, likely a modest positive

The 2025 Tax Act (signed July 4, 2025) reinstated 100% accelerated depreciation and immediate R&D expensing, retroactive to January 2025. This means Amazon can deduct far more capex immediately on its tax return than it recognizes as depreciation expense under GAAP. The result: taxable income is lower than book income → taxes paid in cash are lower than GAAP tax expense → deferred tax liabilities build up → this creates a positive reconciling item in cash flow from operations. Given the scale of Amazon's capex, this could be worth several billion dollars in FY2025.

#### 5. Working Capital Changes: Net Positive (~$5–8B estimated)

Amazon's working capital dynamics consistently *generate* cash because of its negative cash conversion cycle. Here's how:

**Accounts Payable (positive contributor):** Amazon buys inventory and services from suppliers on payment terms (typically 30–90 days). As revenue grows, AP grows — and growing AP is a source of cash because you're receiving goods before paying for them. Amazon had ~$95B in AP at year-end.

**Unearned Revenue (positive contributor):** Prime memberships, AWS prepaid contracts, and gift cards all generate cash before Amazon delivers the service. As Prime prices increase and AWS committed-spend contracts grow, this becomes a larger cash source.

**Inventories (negative contributor):** Growing retail operations require more inventory investment, which consumes cash. However, Amazon manages this tightly through its marketplace model (third-party sellers hold their own inventory) and just-in-time logistics.

**Accounts Receivable (negative contributor):** AWS and advertising revenues carry receivables (customers pay on terms, not immediately). As these higher-margin, B2B revenue streams grow faster than retail, AR grows — consuming cash.

The net working capital effect is typically positive for Amazon because AP growth outpaces AR and inventory growth, thanks to the negative cash conversion cycle. Amazon essentially gets paid by customers before it pays suppliers, which is a structural cash flow advantage.

### The Free Cash Flow Crunch

Now the punchline. Despite generating $139.5B in operating cash flow — a phenomenal number — Amazon's free cash flow collapsed to just $11.2B because capex devoured $128.3B:

| | FY2025 | FY2024 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operating Cash Flow | $139.5B | $115.9B | +$23.6B |
| Capex | ($128.3B) | ($77.7B) | ($50.6B) |
| **Free Cash Flow** | **$11.2B** | **$38.2B** | **($27.0B)** |

Operating cash flow grew by $23.6B, but capex grew by $50.6B — more than double the OCF increase. The incremental capex dollar completely swamped the incremental operating cash dollar.

And it gets worse on a forward-looking basis: Amazon guided to approximately **$100B in capex for 2026** (some estimates range to $200B when including long-term commitments). If OCF grows at a similar 20% rate to ~$167B, FCF would recover to ~$37–67B. But if OCF growth decelerates (say, to 10–15%), or capex runs toward the high end, FCF could remain under severe pressure.

---

### Why This Matters: Amazon's "Cash Flow Machine" Narrative Is Under Strain

For years, Amazon's investment thesis was built on the idea that it's a "cash flow machine" — that even when net income was near zero (2012–2019), OCF was massive and growing. That was true, and it was powered by the same dynamics I described above: heavy depreciation on past capex, SBC, and favorable working capital.

But the AI capex cycle has fundamentally changed the equation. The $128.3B in FY2025 capex represents a step-function increase that will:

1. **Generate enormous future depreciation** — creating a self-reinforcing cycle where OCF looks strong (because depreciation adds back) even if the underlying business doesn't improve proportionally. This can mask deteriorating returns on invested capital.

2. **Compress FCF for years** — even as OCF grows, if capex remains elevated at $100B+/year, FCF will be a fraction of OCF for the foreseeable future.

3. **Shift the risk to AWS** — the vast majority of this capex is for AI/cloud infrastructure. If AWS revenue growth (currently ~24% YoY) decelerates to mid-teens, the return profile on that invested capital deteriorates rapidly.

The key metric to watch going forward isn't OCF (which will look great thanks to depreciation add-backs) — it's **FCF yield** and **return on invested capital (ROIC)**. Amazon's ROIC will come under pressure as the denominator (invested capital) is ballooning at 65% annually while the numerator (operating income) grows at a more modest rate.

```
Net Income                    $77.7B
+ Depreciation & Amortization +$65.8B
+ Stock-Based Compensation    +$19.5B
+ Operating Lease Amortization ~+$11B
+ Deferred Taxes / Other      ~+$3B
± Working Capital Changes     ~+$5-8B

─────────────────────────────────────
= Operating Cash Flow          $139.5B
- Capital Expenditures        ($128.3B)
─────────────────────────────────────
= Free Cash Flow               $11.2B
```

The bottom line: Amazon's operating cash flow engine is running stronger than ever, but the AI infrastructure buildout is consuming nearly all of it. The company is essentially reinvesting 92% of its OCF back into the business — the highest capex intensity in Amazon's history.
